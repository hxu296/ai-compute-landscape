Who powers the Token Economy?

Explore the companies behind AI tokens: their chips, data centers, contracts, prices and inference traffic. Every figure links to its source.

The landscape

How the companies connect

Facility owners lease powered space to cloud operators. Clouds supply AI labs and other clouds. Follow the disclosed links to see who depends on whom.

Swipe across the diagram to follow facilities → clouds → buyers, or choose a company above.

Equal-width links show selected public agreements, including unpriced ones. Motion indicates direction, not volume. Small numbers count counterparties. A connected path does not allocate particular racks or megawatts through a reseller. Undisclosed relationships stay absent.

Most numbers in this business are announced rather than measured. Contract terms can include floors or ceilings, a GPU count is often worked out from megawatts, and a hyperscaler's fleet is somebody else's model. So every figure here carries its source and a tag distinguishing reporting, calculation, and estimation.

How to read the tags
R
Reported by a company, filing, or named press source; this tag is not independent verification.
D
Derived from reported numbers by a stated formula, such as value ÷ term ÷ GPUs.
E
Estimated by an analyst or inferred from indirect evidence. Source ranges are retained where available.

Click a buyer or supplier in the contract flow to see only its deals. Tables sort when you click a column. A source-note marker opens the two dated claims: “source conflict” means unresolved disagreement, “different bases” means the definitions differ, and “later evidence” identifies dated updates. Source-date warnings remain separate from verified revisions.

Why Google’s daily and monthly figures differ

Google’s May 2026 figure of over 3.2 quadrillion tokens/month covers its surfaces broadly (over 106.7T/day using a 30-day month). Tokens are not all the same work

Cache hits apply only to input tokens. This control changes the token composition above; it does not change the rack estimate. The runs do not isolate uncached prefill capacity, so they cannot calibrate a cache-hit-to-rack curve. Rack estimates retain the chosen benchmark’s context and cache behavior.

Assumptions, packing and source checks

Defaults: SemiAnalysis’s public GB200 specifications and ownership model, retrieved November 2, 2026. Annual cost = all physical GPUs × hourly ownership cost × 8,760. Power includes the source’s host, networking and cooling allowance; no extra PUE multiplier is applied. Neither figure is a purchase quote.

The ownership inputs below apply only to the earlier GB200 reference configurations. Costs and facility power for the new hardware scenarios are not estimated.

Method: group equivalents = max(input tokens/day ÷ 86,400 ÷ group input TPS, output tokens/day ÷ 86,400 ÷ group output TPS) ÷ usable capacity. Round up to groups, then to whole racks. This is a fixed-rate extrapolation from coding-trace replay. A different input/output mix, context length, cache distribution or serving stack can change the attainable rates. Full-rack packing was not measured.

Benchmark scope: AgentX variable-length coding traces, prefix caching and speculative decoding. The Flash configurations use different engines and cache setups; this is a configuration comparison, not a controlled model-only comparison. The vLLM Flash run also uses synthetic speculative acceptance. Input throughput counts reused context as well as fresh input.

Why not use the headline? The run pages’ 100-token/s figures interpolate total logical throughput. They are not output throughput. Pro’s overview also applies a decode-share factor after the raw producer has already divided by all allocated GPUs. This calculator uses the raw absolute input and output rates and explicit physical groups instead. Producer · Overview transformation.

Unresolved source metadata: Pro’s API labels precision “fp4”; inspected worker logs name deepseek_v4_fp8 with bfloat16 dtype. The API’s October 14 date also differs from the September run timestamps. These fields do not establish uniform arithmetic precision or an exact measurement date. The selected older GB200 values are preserved in the original snapshot; new configurations, full raw rows, response hashes and the H100 count correction are in the expanded snapshot.

The earlier 40T/day example can be reproduced with 2% output, 100% usable capacity and Pro result 444911: 244 racks. Its 60 allocated GPUs per rack include both prefill and decode; only 36 are decode GPUs. This configuration reaches 95.8, rather than 100, tokens/s/user at P90 latency.

The historical Fireworks Pro 0813 serverless tariff is not used as current revenue: Fireworks says that endpoint was retired from serverless. Current GPU ownership cost and hypothetical API billing are different quantities.

Demand

Efficiency, training and financing

Efficient open-model inference can need far fewer racks than a raw token headline suggests. Whether this creates surplus supply also depends on demand, task quality, training, delivery schedules and financing.

The small-rack counterargument

These are attributed social posts, read through public transcriptions because direct X retrieval was blocked. The expanded raw Flash/GB300 benchmark above gives 214 racks at 100% reference capacity or 305 at 70% for an assumed 500T/day with 2% output. This supports the scale of the thought experiment under those assumptions. It does not establish a measured global total or equal-quality replacement for every task. Claims, replies and arithmetic.

Applied Compute’s training loop

Applied Compute reports more than 1,700 training runs since July and more than 1T inference tokens/day in its November 2 update. These are separate activity measures, with no disclosed GPU-hours or repeat-customer retention series. Its August 28 Kimi K3 engineering account identifies memory, rollout precision, weight synchronization and checkpoint I/O constraints; optimizations reportedly cut GPUs per training replica by about 40%. Modal’s May 20 customer account describes environment startup and parallel grading as ways CPU work can leave GPUs idle. These show engineering bottlenecks, not proof of a current industry-wide GPU shortage.

Where training demand can persist

Post-training couples rollout generation, weight updates and evaluation. Applied systems can repeatedly retrain as production data, rewards and base models change. Reward quality, environment speed, numerical alignment, networking and deployment can bottleneck the loop; more GPU-hours solve only some of these. LoRA and better base models can also shrink individual jobs. Recurring demand is plausible, but its aggregate size and duration are not established.

Fireworks’ August 31 account describes specialized training and experience running RL across more than 10,000 GPUs. Nebius’ Q3 notes add workload-specific speculative-model training. Product and vendor evidence does not measure market-wide utilization.

What Anthropic’s filing says about compute allocation

For January–September 2026, the November amendment reports $18.808B compute and infrastructure expense. Note 8 separately reports $4.812B cost of revenue and $19.240B R&D. Cost of revenue is mainly customer-serving compute plus some staff/overhead; R&D includes model development, research, internal use, personnel and overhead. These are overlapping accounting classifications, not quantities to add or GPU-hour shares. Anthropic describes dynamically allocating a common compute pool. Neither “all R&D is pretraining” nor “stopping training immediately releases its entire fleet” follows. November S-1 amendment, Note 8 and compute discussion.

Lab liquidity and runway limits

Anthropic liquidity stress scenario

This uses a dated gross liquidity pool and user assumptions. It does not model future revenue, commitments, additional restrictions, financing or the maturity and terms of obligations. Reported positive operating cash flow is not a promise of future self-funding.

Training runs, cluster access and owned chips

Access to a cluster is different from a simultaneous model run, owned chips, delivered chips or future commitments. The largest current run is often undisclosed; historical examples remain labeled.

Training cutbacks could weaken marginal rents if released, compatible capacity exceeds incremental demand. Contract cancellation rights, repayments, power, rack readiness and migrations determine when that capacity becomes sellable. Track idle capacity and same-SKU realized prices rather than treating a spot quote or a forecast as a market-wide collapse.
