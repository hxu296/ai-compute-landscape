Who supplies AI compute, at what price, and for what

Three kinds of company now sell GPU capacity: the hyperscalers, the neoclouds that rent GPUs, and the power hosts that build the halls. This guide lines up their fleets, contracts, prices and growth, one question at a time.

Most numbers in this business are announced rather than measured. A contract value is a ceiling, a GPU count is often worked out from megawatts, and a hyperscaler's fleet is somebody else's model. So every figure here carries its source and a tag saying how much to trust it.

How to read the tags
R
Reported by the company or in a filing.
D
Derived from reported numbers by a stated formula, such as value ÷ term ÷ GPUs.
E
Estimated from indirect evidence, such as GPUs inferred from power. Shown as a range.

01Fleets

Google has the largest fleet, and most of it is its own chips

No hyperscaler says how many accelerators it owns. The best public estimate is Epoch AI's model of chip deliveries, which converts every chip into H100-equivalents so that a TPU and a GPU can share an axis. Neoclouds and hosts rarely disclose GPU counts either, but they do disclose power, so the second view compares them in megawatts.

02Contracts

Two AI labs signed most of the compute contracts

A compute contract is a promise to pay for capacity over several years. The flow below runs from buyer to supplier, and each band's width is the disclosed value. Framework deals such as Stargate's $500B plan are left out, because they commit no one to a price.
